Kidney Stones

Kidney stones are one of the most painful conditions in urology — and one of the most treatable. Dr. Hamoui offers the full range of stone management options.

Home › Conditions › Kidney Stones

What Are Kidney Stones?

Kidney stones (nephrolithiasis) are hard mineral and salt deposits that form inside the kidneys when urine becomes too concentrated and minerals crystallize. Stones can range in size from a grain of sand to a golf ball. Small stones often pass on their own; larger stones may require intervention.

Approximately 1 in 10 Americans will develop a kidney stone at some point in their lifetime. Kidney stones are the third most common urologic condition after urinary tract infections and BPH. Once you've had one stone, the risk of recurrence is approximately 50% within 5–10 years without preventive measures.

There are four main types of kidney stones: calcium oxalate (most common, ~80%), uric acid (~5–10%), struvite (infection-related, ~10%), and cystine (rare, hereditary).

Common Symptoms

  • Severe, cramping pain in the back, side, and lower abdomen (renal colic)
  • Pain that radiates to the groin as the stone moves down the ureter
  • Pain that comes in waves and fluctuates in intensity
  • Blood in the urine (hematuria) — pink, red, or brown discoloration
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Frequent or painful urination
  • Cloudy or foul-smelling urine
  • Fever and chills (if infection is present)

Kidney stones in the kidney itself may be completely asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on imaging. Symptoms typically begin when the stone moves into the ureter.

Causes & Risk Factors

  • Inadequate fluid intake: The most common modifiable risk factor — concentrated urine promotes crystallization
  • High dietary oxalate: Found in spinach, nuts, chocolate, and tea — contributes to calcium oxalate stones
  • High dietary sodium: Increases urinary calcium excretion
  • High animal protein intake: Increases uric acid and reduces urinary citrate (a natural stone inhibitor)
  • Obesity and metabolic syndrome: Strongly linked to uric acid and calcium stone formation
  • Hyperparathyroidism: Elevated PTH causes high urinary calcium
  • Inflammatory bowel disease or prior bowel surgery: Increases oxalate absorption
  • Family history: Genetic predisposition is present in many stone formers
  • Prior kidney stones: Most predictive risk factor for recurrence

Treatment Options

Medical Expulsive Therapy

Small stones (<5–6 mm) have a high rate of spontaneous passage. Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) relax the ureter to facilitate stone passage and reduce pain. Hydration, pain management (NSAIDs, ketorolac), and close monitoring are the foundation of conservative management. Dr. Hamoui guides patients through this process with clear instructions on when to return if passage fails.

Shockwave Lithotripsy (SWL)

Non-invasive shockwave lithotripsy uses focused sound waves to fragment stones into passable pieces. Performed on an outpatient basis, it is most effective for kidney stones ≤2 cm that are accessible to shockwaves. SWL requires no incisions and minimal recovery. Multiple sessions may be needed.

Ureteroscopy with Laser Lithotripsy (URS)

A thin scope is passed through the urethra and bladder into the ureter or kidney to directly visualize and laser-fragment stones. A ureteral stent is typically placed temporarily afterward. URS is highly effective for ureteral stones of any size and kidney stones up to 2 cm. It is the most commonly performed stone procedure and has excellent stone-free rates.

Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy (PCNL)

For large (≥2 cm), complex, or staghorn stones, PCNL provides direct access to the kidney through a small flank incision, allowing complete stone removal. Dr. Hamoui's dual training in urology and interventional radiology uniquely equips him to safely navigate this technically demanding procedure. PCNL achieves the highest stone-free rates of any modality for large-burden stones.

When to See a Doctor

  • Go to the emergency room if you have fever with severe flank pain (possible infected obstructed kidney — a urologic emergency)
  • Severe, uncontrolled pain requiring IV medications
  • Unable to keep fluids down due to vomiting
  • Stone not passing after 4–6 weeks of watchful waiting
  • Reduced kidney function or single kidney
  • Blood in urine without prior stone diagnosis
  • Recurrent stone episodes — metabolic evaluation can identify and correct preventable causes
