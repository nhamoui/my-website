Urinary Tract Infections

UTIs are among the most common infections — but recurrent or complicated UTIs require specialist evaluation to identify and address underlying causes.

Home › Conditions › Urinary Tract Infections

What Are UTIs?

A urinary tract infection (UTI) is a bacterial infection of any part of the urinary tract — the kidneys, ureters, bladder, or urethra. Most UTIs involve the lower tract (bladder and urethra). An estimated 8–10 million Americans visit doctors each year for UTIs, making them the second most common type of infection overall.

UTIs are far more common in women due to anatomical differences (shorter urethra), but men with urologic abnormalities — particularly BPH, prostatitis, or bladder stones — are also significantly affected. A UTI in a man is always considered complicated and warrants urologic evaluation.

  • Cystitis: Infection of the bladder — most common type
  • Urethritis: Infection of the urethra — often associated with STIs
  • Pyelonephritis: Infection of the kidney — more serious, requires aggressive treatment

Common Symptoms

  • Burning or pain with urination (dysuria)
  • Frequent urge to urinate, often passing small amounts
  • Cloudy, dark, or foul-smelling urine
  • Blood in the urine (hematuria)
  • Pelvic pressure or lower abdominal discomfort
  • Fever, chills, nausea, and back/flank pain (kidney infection — pyelonephritis)
  • Confusion or altered mental status in older adults

Fever and systemic symptoms suggest upper tract involvement (pyelonephritis) and require more aggressive evaluation and treatment than uncomplicated cystitis.

Causes & Risk Factors

Most UTIs are caused by Escherichia coli (E. coli) from the bowel flora. Other common pathogens include Klebsiella, Proteus, Staphylococcus saprophyticus (young women), and Enterococcus.

Risk factors in women:

  • Sexual activity (new or frequent)
  • Menopause and estrogen deficiency (urethral/vaginal changes)
  • Diaphragm use with spermicide
  • Prior UTI history

Risk factors in men:

  • BPH with urinary retention (incomplete emptying)
  • Prostatitis
  • Kidney or bladder stones
  • Urinary catheter use
  • Immunosuppression (diabetes, HIV)

Recurrent UTI risk factors (either sex):

  • Urinary tract anatomical abnormalities
  • Incomplete bladder emptying
  • Bladder stones or foreign bodies
  • Prior antibiotic courses selecting for resistant organisms

Treatment Options

Antibiotic Therapy

Uncomplicated cystitis is treated with a short course of antibiotics — nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or fosfomycin are preferred. Fluoroquinolones are reserved for more complex infections. Urine culture guides selection when resistance is suspected or prior treatment has failed. Pyelonephritis typically requires 7–14 days of therapy and IV antibiotics if hospitalization is needed.

Recurrent UTI Prevention

For women with recurrent UTIs (≥3/year or ≥2 in 6 months), preventive strategies include post-coital antibiotic prophylaxis, low-dose daily prophylaxis, patient-initiated self-treatment, and topical vaginal estrogen (in postmenopausal women). Cranberry products and D-mannose have modest supporting evidence and no side effects — Dr. Hamoui discusses evidence with patients directly.

Treating Underlying Causes

Recurrent or complicated UTIs in men or women with structural abnormalities require addressing the root cause: BPH treatment to restore complete bladder emptying, stone removal, correction of urethral stricture, repair of vesicovaginal fistula, or management of bladder dysfunction with incomplete emptying. Dr. Hamoui performs urologic evaluation — imaging, cystoscopy, and urodynamics as appropriate — to identify predisposing anatomy.

Cystoscopy & Evaluation

Persistent hematuria with UTI, or recurrent UTIs in men, warrants cystoscopic evaluation of the bladder to rule out stones, foreign bodies, bladder lesions, or anatomic abnormalities. Imaging (CT urogram, renal ultrasound) is used to evaluate the upper tracts. Dr. Hamoui performs in-office flexible cystoscopy to provide rapid, comfortable evaluation.

When to See a Doctor

  • Seek urgent care for high fever, chills, severe back pain with UTI symptoms — possible pyelonephritis or urosepsis
  • Any UTI in a man (always warrants evaluation for underlying cause)
  • Blood in the urine accompanying UTI symptoms
  • Recurrent UTIs (≥2–3 per year)
  • UTI symptoms that do not clear with a standard antibiotic course
  • Urinary symptoms that persist after completing antibiotics (possible antibiotic resistance)
