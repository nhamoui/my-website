Urinary Incontinence

Involuntary urine loss affects millions of Americans — but it is not something you simply have to accept. Modern urology offers highly effective solutions.

Home › Conditions › Urinary Incontinence

What Is Urinary Incontinence?

Urinary incontinence is the involuntary loss of urine. It is a symptom, not a disease — and it has multiple distinct types, each with different mechanisms and treatments. Accurate classification is the first and most important step toward effective management.

The main types include:

  • Stress urinary incontinence (SUI): Leakage triggered by physical activity — coughing, sneezing, laughing, lifting, or exercise — due to weak urethral sphincter or pelvic floor
  • Urge urinary incontinence (UUI): Leakage preceded by a sudden strong urge; caused by overactive detrusor (bladder wall muscle) contractions
  • Mixed incontinence: Features of both stress and urge incontinence (very common)
  • Overflow incontinence: Bladder never fully empties; chronic overfilling leads to dribbling leakage; more common in men with BPH or neurogenic bladder
  • Functional incontinence: Physical or cognitive impairment prevents timely toileting

Common Symptoms

  • Urine leakage during coughing, sneezing, laughing, or exercise (stress)
  • Strong sudden urge to urinate followed by leakage before reaching toilet (urge)
  • Frequent urination (>8 times/day) or nighttime urination
  • Inability to fully empty the bladder, with dribbling (overflow)
  • Constant wetness or dampness (continuous leakage)
  • Skin irritation from chronic urine contact

Causes & Risk Factors

Stress incontinence causes:

  • Pregnancy and vaginal delivery (pelvic floor injury)
  • Menopause (estrogen-related tissue changes)
  • Radical prostatectomy (sphincter injury in men)
  • Obesity (increased intra-abdominal pressure)

Urge incontinence causes:

  • Overactive bladder (detrusor overactivity)
  • Neurological conditions (stroke, Parkinson's, MS)
  • Bladder outlet obstruction (BPH in men)
  • Bladder irritants (caffeine, alcohol, infections)

Overflow incontinence causes:

  • BPH causing bladder outlet obstruction
  • Diabetic neuropathy or other neurogenic bladder
  • Medications that impair bladder contraction

Treatment Options

Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation

Kegel exercises and pelvic floor physical therapy are first-line for both stress and urge incontinence. Biofeedback-guided therapy improves muscle coordination and sphincter control. Bladder training (scheduled voiding, urge suppression techniques) is highly effective for urge incontinence. These approaches have no side effects and provide durable benefit.

Medications

For urge incontinence/OAB: anticholinergics (oxybutynin, solifenacin) or beta-3 agonists (mirabegron). For stress incontinence in women: topical vaginal estrogen improves urethral tissue. For post-prostatectomy incontinence: duloxetine (off-label) may help mild cases. Dr. Hamoui reviews all medications including side effects and interactions carefully.

Minimally Invasive Procedures

For stress incontinence: mid-urethral slings (retropubic or transobturator) are the gold-standard surgical treatment for women, restoring continence in 80–90% of cases. For women, bulking agents (urethral injections) offer a minimally invasive office option. Bladder Botox and tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) are effective for urge incontinence refractory to medication.

Artificial Urinary Sphincter (AUS)

For men with significant stress incontinence after prostate surgery, the artificial urinary sphincter (AUS) is the gold-standard surgical treatment. This implantable device provides voluntary control over urination with high patient satisfaction rates exceeding 85–90%. The male urethral sling (AdVance) is an alternative for mild to moderate post-prostatectomy incontinence.

When to See a Doctor

  • Leakage is occurring regularly and affecting your confidence or activities
  • You are limiting fluid intake, avoiding exercise, or wearing protective pads every day
  • You have blood in your urine (needs evaluation)
  • Incontinence developed suddenly after surgery or a neurological event
  • You are not emptying your bladder fully (overflow symptoms)
  • Self-treatment such as Kegels alone has not improved symptoms after 3 months
