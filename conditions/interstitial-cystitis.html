Interstitial Cystitis

Chronic bladder pain and urgency without infection requires a specialist's care — IC is real, diagnosable, and manageable with the right approach.

Home › Conditions › Interstitial Cystitis

What Is Interstitial Cystitis?

Interstitial cystitis (IC), also called bladder pain syndrome (BPS), is a chronic condition characterized by recurring bladder pain, pressure, or discomfort — often accompanied by urinary urgency and frequency — in the absence of a urinary tract infection or other identifiable cause. IC affects an estimated 3–8 million women in the United States (and a smaller but significant number of men) and is frequently misdiagnosed as recurrent UTIs for years before the correct diagnosis is made.

IC is a diagnosis of exclusion — infections, bladder cancer, radiation cystitis, and other conditions that mimic its symptoms must be ruled out. Two subtypes exist: ulcerative IC (Hunner lesions on the bladder wall, ~10% of cases) and non-ulcerative IC (the majority). This distinction is clinically important because treatment response differs.

IC vs. OAB: IC is characterized by pain as a primary symptom; OAB (overactive bladder) is characterized by urgency without pain. Some patients have overlapping features. Accurate distinction guides treatment selection.

Common Symptoms

  • Chronic pelvic or bladder pain, pressure, or discomfort
  • Pain that worsens as the bladder fills and temporarily improves after voiding
  • Urinary urgency — a strong need to void to relieve pain, not just pressure
  • Urinary frequency — often voiding 10–60 times per day in severe cases
  • Nocturia — frequent awakening at night to void
  • Pain during or after sexual intercourse (dyspareunia)
  • Worsening symptoms around the menstrual cycle
  • Flares triggered by specific foods, stress, prolonged sitting, or sexual activity

IC symptoms wax and wane. Flares can be triggered by stress, diet (particularly acidic foods, caffeine, alcohol, artificial sweeteners), hormonal changes, and other factors unique to each patient.

Causes & Risk Factors

The cause of IC is not fully understood, but leading theories involve:

  • Defective bladder epithelium (GAG layer): The protective lining of the bladder becomes leaky, allowing urinary irritants to penetrate and trigger an inflammatory response
  • Immune/inflammatory dysregulation: Elevated mast cells and inflammatory mediators in the bladder wall
  • Central sensitization: Altered pain processing in the nervous system — IC frequently co-occurs with fibromyalgia, IBS, vulvodynia, and chronic fatigue syndrome

Risk factors include:

  • Female sex (IC is 5–10× more common in women)
  • Age 30–50 at presentation
  • History of prior UTIs or pelvic inflammatory disease
  • Chronic pain syndromes (fibromyalgia, IBS, vulvodynia)
  • Autoimmune conditions
  • Prior pelvic trauma or surgery

Treatment Options

IC management is multimodal and individualized. No single treatment works for everyone; layering approaches improves outcomes. Dr. Hamoui develops a personalized care plan for each patient.

Dietary Modification & Self-Management

Identifying and eliminating personal food and drink triggers is a cornerstone of IC management. Common triggers include acidic foods (citrus, tomatoes), caffeine, alcohol, artificial sweeteners, and spicy foods. The IC diet is a starting framework that patients customize. Stress reduction, pelvic floor physical therapy, and bladder training also play important roles in symptom control.

Oral Medications

Pentosan polysulfate sodium (Elmiron) — the only FDA-approved oral medication for IC — is thought to help restore the protective bladder lining. It requires 3–6 months for full effect. Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline) reduce bladder pain through central pain modulation and improve sleep. Antihistamines (hydroxyzine) target mast cell activity. Gabapentinoids address central sensitization. Dr. Hamoui tailors oral therapy to each patient's symptom profile.

Intravesical Therapy

Bladder instillations — medications placed directly into the bladder via catheter — bypass the systemic circulation and act locally. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) is FDA-approved; heparin, lidocaine, and sodium bicarbonate "cocktails" are commonly used off-label to reduce inflammation and restore the GAG layer. A series of in-office instillations can produce significant symptom relief for many patients.

Cystoscopy with Hydrodistension & Hunner Lesion Treatment

Cystoscopy under anesthesia with bladder hydrodistension (filling the bladder under pressure) confirms the diagnosis and provides relief lasting months in some patients. In the subset with Hunner lesions (ulcerative IC), direct fulguration or injection of the lesions with triamcinolone significantly reduces pain and frequency. Identification of Hunner lesions changes both prognosis and treatment — making diagnostic cystoscopy important in refractory cases.

When to See a Doctor

  • Pelvic or bladder pain is present for more than 6 weeks without a documented infection
  • Multiple urine cultures have been negative yet UTI-like symptoms persist
  • Urinary frequency is significantly exceeding 8 times per day
  • Pain with intercourse is affecting intimacy and quality of life
  • Symptoms are waxing and waning with dietary or stress patterns
  • You have been told "everything is normal" but continue to suffer — IC requires a specialist familiar with its nuances
