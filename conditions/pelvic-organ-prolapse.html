Pelvic Organ Prolapse

When pelvic organs shift out of their normal position, it can cause significant discomfort and urinary symptoms — but highly effective treatments exist at every stage.

Home › Conditions › Pelvic Organ Prolapse

What Is Pelvic Organ Prolapse?

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) occurs when the muscles, ligaments, and connective tissues of the pelvic floor — which support the bladder, uterus, vagina, and rectum — become weakened or damaged, allowing one or more pelvic organs to descend into or beyond the vaginal canal. Approximately 1 in 4 women has some degree of prolapse, though not all cases require intervention.

Types of prolapse are named by the organ involved:

  • Cystocele (anterior prolapse): Bladder drops into the front vaginal wall — the most common type
  • Rectocele (posterior prolapse): Rectum bulges into the back vaginal wall
  • Uterine prolapse: Uterus descends into the vaginal canal
  • Vaginal vault prolapse: Upper vagina collapses downward after hysterectomy
  • Enterocele: Small intestine descends between the vagina and rectum

Common Symptoms

  • A sensation of pelvic pressure, heaviness, or "something falling out"
  • A visible or palpable bulge at or outside the vaginal opening
  • Urinary symptoms: leakage, urgency, frequency, or difficulty emptying the bladder
  • Need to manually reduce the prolapse to void or have a bowel movement
  • Difficulty with bowel movements (incomplete emptying, straining)
  • Low back or pelvic aching that worsens with prolonged standing or activity
  • Discomfort or reduced sensation during sexual intercourse

Symptoms are typically worse after standing for long periods and often resolve or improve when lying down. Mild prolapse may be asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during a routine exam.

Causes & Risk Factors

  • Vaginal childbirth: The primary risk factor — especially prolonged labor, large babies, or instrumental delivery (forceps/vacuum)
  • Menopause: Estrogen loss weakens pelvic floor tissues and supportive ligaments
  • Aging: Progressive connective tissue laxity
  • Obesity: Sustained increased intra-abdominal pressure
  • Chronic straining: Constipation, heavy lifting, or chronic cough
  • Prior pelvic surgery: Hysterectomy increases risk of vaginal vault prolapse
  • Connective tissue disorders: Ehlers-Danlos syndrome and similar conditions
  • Family history: Genetic component to pelvic floor structure

Treatment Options

Not all prolapse requires surgery. Treatment is guided by the degree of prolapse, symptom severity, and patient goals — particularly regarding future pregnancies and sexual activity.

Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

For mild to moderate prolapse, supervised pelvic floor physical therapy strengthens and coordinates the muscles that support the pelvic organs. Biofeedback-guided Kegel training, along with education on bowel habits and intra-abdominal pressure management, can reduce symptoms significantly. This is first-line for women who are not yet candidates for or interested in surgical repair.

Pessary

A vaginal pessary is a removable silicone device inserted into the vagina to mechanically support prolapsed organs. Available in many shapes and sizes, a correctly fitted pessary can provide excellent symptom relief without surgery. Suitable for women of any age — particularly those who prefer to avoid surgery, are awaiting surgery, or are still completing childbearing. Dr. Hamoui fits and manages pessaries in the office.

Native Tissue Repair

Surgical prolapse repair using the body's own tissues (native tissue repair) corrects the defect by reattaching or reinforcing the weakened pelvic floor support. Anterior colporrhaphy repairs cystoceles; posterior colporrhaphy repairs rectoceles; uterosacral or sacrospinous ligament suspension corrects apical (vault) prolapse. Native tissue repair carries low risk and can be performed through a vaginal approach.

Minimally Invasive Sacrocolpopexy

Robotic or laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy — attaching the vaginal vault to the sacrum via a mesh bridge — provides the most durable repair for apical prolapse and is the preferred approach for younger, more active women. As a robotic surgery specialist, Dr. Hamoui performs this procedure with precision through small incisions, offering excellent long-term outcomes with faster recovery than open surgery.

When to See a Doctor

  • You feel pelvic pressure, heaviness, or a bulge vaginally
  • Symptoms are worsening with standing or physical activity
  • You have difficulty emptying your bladder or bowels
  • Prolapse is affecting your sexual activity or comfort
  • You have urinary incontinence or urgency that may be related to bladder prolapse
  • You want a non-surgical option to manage symptoms while you consider your choices
