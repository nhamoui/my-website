Men's Health

Enlarged Prostate (BPH)

Benign prostatic hyperplasia is the most common prostate condition in men over 50 — and very manageable with today's treatment options.

Home › Conditions › Enlarged Prostate (BPH)

What Is BPH?

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a non-cancerous enlargement of the prostate gland. The prostate surrounds the urethra — the tube that carries urine from the bladder — so as it enlarges, it can compress and obstruct urinary flow. BPH is the most common benign tumor in men and affects more than half of men over age 60 and up to 90% of men in their 80s.

BPH is not prostate cancer and does not increase the risk of developing cancer. However, it can significantly impair quality of life through urinary symptoms and, if left untreated, may lead to bladder and kidney complications over time.

Common Symptoms

BPH causes lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) that range from mildly bothersome to severely disruptive:

  • Weak or slow urinary stream
  • Difficulty starting urination (hesitancy)
  • Straining to urinate
  • Feeling of incomplete bladder emptying
  • Frequent urination, especially at night (nocturia)
  • Urgency — sudden, difficult-to-control urge to urinate
  • Dribbling at the end of urination
  • Urinary tract infections due to incomplete emptying

In severe cases, BPH can cause acute urinary retention — a sudden inability to urinate that requires emergency catheterization.

Causes & Risk Factors

The exact cause of BPH is not fully understood, but hormonal changes that occur with aging — particularly the accumulation of dihydrotestosterone (DHT) — play a central role in driving prostate cell growth.

  • Age: BPH is uncommon before 40 but becomes progressively more prevalent thereafter
  • Family history: Men with a first-degree relative with BPH have a higher risk
  • Obesity: Higher body mass index is associated with increased BPH risk
  • Lack of physical activity: Regular exercise reduces BPH risk
  • Diabetes and heart disease: Associated with higher rates of BPH and LUTS
  • Ethnicity: BPH is more common in Western populations

Treatment Options

Treatment depends on symptom severity, prostate size, and patient preference. Dr. Hamoui offers a full spectrum of medical and procedural options.

Watchful Waiting & Lifestyle Changes

Men with mild symptoms may benefit from behavioral modifications: limiting fluids before bed, reducing caffeine and alcohol, and performing timed voiding. Regular monitoring ensures any progression is caught early. This is appropriate for men whose symptoms are not significantly impacting quality of life.

Medications

Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin) relax the muscle of the prostate and bladder neck to improve urinary flow — often effective within days. 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride, dutasteride) shrink the prostate over 3–6 months. Combination therapy is often used for larger prostates. PDE5 inhibitors (tadalafil) are FDA-approved for BPH/LUTS as well.

Minimally Invasive Procedures

For men who fail medications or prefer non-surgical office-based solutions, minimally invasive therapies offer excellent symptom relief. Options include UroLift (prostatic urethral lift), Rezūm water vapor therapy, and prostatic artery embolization (PAE) — a catheter-based procedure Dr. Hamoui is uniquely qualified to perform through his interventional radiology training, allowing prostate shrinkage without general anesthesia or incisions.

Surgical Options

When medications and minimally invasive procedures are insufficient, surgery delivers the most durable results. TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) remains the gold standard for moderate-to-large prostates. Laser procedures such as HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation) and GreenLight PVP offer reduced bleeding and faster recovery. Robotic simple prostatectomy is used for very large glands.

When to See a Doctor

  • Urinary symptoms are affecting your sleep, daily activities, or quality of life
  • You are getting up more than once per night to urinate
  • You feel you cannot completely empty your bladder
  • You have had a urinary tract infection related to incomplete emptying
  • You are suddenly unable to urinate (seek emergency care immediately)
  • You have blood in your urine (rule out other causes)
