Oncology

Testicular Cancer

The most common cancer in men ages 15–35. Highly curable even when advanced — prompt evaluation and treatment are essential.

Home›Conditions›Testicular Cancer

What Is Testicular Cancer?

Testicular cancer develops in the cells of one or both testicles. The two main types are seminomas (slow-growing, very radiation-sensitive) and non-seminomas (faster-growing, including embryonal carcinoma, teratoma, choriocarcinoma, and yolk sac tumors). Mixed germ cell tumors contain elements of both types.

Despite being the most common solid tumor in men ages 15–35, testicular cancer has one of the highest cure rates of any cancer — including in advanced stages — because it responds remarkably well to chemotherapy and radiation. Early evaluation of any testicular lump is critical.

Common Symptoms

A painless lump or swelling in one testicle — the most common sign
Dull ache or heaviness in the lower abdomen, back, or scrotum
A feeling of pressure in the scrotum
Sudden accumulation of fluid in the scrotum (hydrocele)
Breast tenderness or growth (gynecomastia) from hormones released by the tumor
Back pain (from enlarged retroperitoneal lymph nodes)
Fatigue and unexplained weight loss
Shortness of breath or cough (if spread to lungs)

Causes & Risk Factors

Undescended Testicle

Cryptorchidism is the strongest known risk factor, even if surgically corrected.

Family History

Brothers or fathers with testicular cancer increase individual risk significantly.

Personal History

Men who have had cancer in one testicle have increased risk in the other.

Age & Race

Most common between ages 15–35; white men are at higher risk than other groups.

Klinefelter Syndrome

This genetic condition (extra X chromosome) is associated with elevated risk.

Carcinoma In Situ (CIS)

Intratubular germ cell neoplasia is a pre-malignant condition that frequently progresses to cancer.

Treatment Options

Surgical

Radical Orchiectomy

  • Removal of the affected testicle — the first step for all testicular cancers
  • Performed through an inguinal (groin) incision
  • Provides definitive diagnosis and pathologic staging
  • Testicular prosthesis available for cosmetic replacement
Surveillance

Active Surveillance (Stage I)

  • Close monitoring with tumor markers and CT imaging
  • Avoids radiation or chemotherapy toxicity for low-risk patients
  • Requires excellent patient compliance with follow-up
  • Recommended for many Stage IA seminomas and non-seminomas
Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy (BEP)

  • Bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin (BEP) regimen
  • Highly effective for metastatic germ cell tumors
  • Curative even in advanced stages in most patients
  • Coordinated with medical oncology
Surgical

Retroperitoneal Lymph Node Dissection (RPLND)

  • Removal of lymph nodes behind the abdomen for staging/treatment
  • Performed for certain Stage II non-seminomas
  • Can be curative without chemotherapy in selected patients
  • Robotic approach available at select centers

Seek Evaluation Promptly

  • Any new, painless lump or firmness in a testicle — do not wait to "see if it goes away"
  • A testicle that has changed in size or shape
  • Unexplained scrotal heaviness, dull ache, or feeling of pressure
  • You have a history of undescended testicle and have never been screened
  • Breast enlargement or tenderness in a young man without explanation
