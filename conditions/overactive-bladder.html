Overactive Bladder

Urgent, frequent urination doesn't have to control your life. Highly effective treatments — from behavioral strategies to advanced procedures — are available.

What Is Overactive Bladder?

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a syndrome characterized by a sudden, strong urge to urinate that is difficult to control. It affects approximately 33 million Americans and is more common with advancing age — though it is not a normal or inevitable part of aging. OAB significantly impairs quality of life, affecting work, sleep, travel, and social activities.

OAB is caused by involuntary contractions of the detrusor (bladder wall muscle) during bladder filling, even when the bladder is not full. It may occur with or without urge incontinence (leakage). OAB without leakage is sometimes called "dry OAB"; with leakage it is "wet OAB."

OAB is a clinical diagnosis — not a disease with a single identifiable cause. It is important to rule out infection, bladder cancer, stones, and other mimicking conditions before accepting an OAB diagnosis.

Common Symptoms

  • Sudden, strong urge to urinate that is hard to defer
  • Urinating 8 or more times per day
  • Waking up 2 or more times per night to urinate (nocturia)
  • Urge urinary incontinence — leaking urine before reaching the toilet
  • Rushing to the bathroom and not always making it in time
  • Planning activities around bathroom access

Causes & Risk Factors

OAB often has no single identifiable cause, but contributing factors include:

  • Aging: Bladder capacity decreases and detrusor overactivity increases with age
  • Neurological conditions: Stroke, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury can disrupt bladder control signals
  • BPH in men: Bladder outlet obstruction can trigger secondary detrusor overactivity
  • Pelvic floor dysfunction: Weak or uncoordinated pelvic muscles contribute to urgency
  • Caffeine, alcohol, and artificial sweeteners: Known bladder irritants that worsen urgency
  • Obesity: Increased intra-abdominal pressure and bladder irritation
  • Prior pelvic surgery or radiation
  • Urinary tract infections: Can trigger or worsen urgency symptoms

Treatment Options

OAB treatment follows a stepwise approach, starting with behavioral strategies and escalating for refractory cases.

Behavioral Therapy & Lifestyle Changes

First-line therapy includes bladder training (scheduled voiding with progressively extended intervals), fluid management, and eliminating bladder irritants (caffeine, alcohol, acidic foods). Pelvic floor physical therapy with biofeedback improves muscle coordination. These approaches have no side effects and can produce substantial improvement in urgency and frequency.

Medications (Anticholinergics & Beta-3 Agonists)

Anticholinergic medications (oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, darifenacin) reduce involuntary bladder contractions. Beta-3 adrenergic agonists (mirabegron, vibegron) relax the bladder with a different mechanism and fewer anticholinergic side effects — particularly relevant in older patients. Dr. Hamoui tailors medication selection based on comorbidities and tolerance.

Bladder Botox (OnabotulinumtoxinA)

Cystoscopic injection of Botox directly into the bladder wall is highly effective for refractory OAB — reducing urgency episodes by 50–70%. Performed as a brief office procedure under local anesthesia, effects last 6–9 months and can be repeated. FDA-approved for OAB. A small percentage of patients require temporary self-catheterization afterward if voiding becomes difficult.

Neuromodulation

For patients who fail medications and Botox, neuromodulation therapies modulate bladder nerve signals. Sacral neuromodulation (Interstim) involves a small implantable device that delivers mild electrical stimulation to the sacral nerves. Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) is an in-office, needle-free option requiring a series of weekly sessions. Both are highly effective for appropriately selected patients.

When to See a Doctor

  • Urgency or frequency is disrupting work, sleep, or social activities
  • You are experiencing urge leakage (urge incontinence)
  • You have blood in your urine (needs evaluation before OAB treatment)
  • Symptoms developed suddenly or recently — consider infection or other cause
  • Over-the-counter bladder supplements or dietary changes haven't helped
  • Prior medication trials have not provided adequate relief
