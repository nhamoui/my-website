Men's Health

Low Testosterone

Testosterone deficiency is a common, underdiagnosed condition that affects energy, mood, body composition, and sexual health — and responds well to treatment.

Home › Conditions › Low Testosterone

What Is Low Testosterone?

Low testosterone, or male hypogonadism, is a condition in which the testes do not produce sufficient testosterone to maintain normal male physiology. Testosterone is the primary male sex hormone, playing critical roles in libido, erectile function, muscle mass, bone density, mood, energy, and red blood cell production.

Testosterone levels naturally decline with age — by approximately 1–2% per year after age 30. However, when levels fall below the normal range and cause symptoms, it is a medical condition that warrants evaluation and treatment. An estimated 4–5 million American men have hypogonadism, yet only a fraction are diagnosed and treated.

Diagnosis requires both: low morning serum testosterone (typically <300 ng/dL on two separate tests) AND the presence of clinical symptoms. Lab values alone are not sufficient to diagnose or treat.

Common Symptoms

  • Reduced sex drive (decreased libido)
  • Erectile dysfunction
  • Fatigue and decreased energy
  • Depressed mood, irritability, difficulty concentrating
  • Loss of muscle mass and increased body fat (especially abdominal)
  • Decreased bone mineral density (osteopenia/osteoporosis)
  • Reduced body and facial hair
  • Breast tissue development (gynecomastia)
  • Reduced volume of ejaculate
  • Anemia (low red blood cell count)
  • Sleep disturbances

Causes & Risk Factors

Low testosterone can arise from problems in the testes (primary hypogonadism) or from problems in the brain's signaling to the testes (secondary hypogonadism).

Primary causes (testicular):

  • Age-related decline (late-onset hypogonadism)
  • Klinefelter syndrome (extra X chromosome)
  • Prior testicular injury, infection (orchitis), or torsion
  • Chemotherapy or radiation to the testes
  • Prior orchiectomy (testicular cancer treatment)

Secondary causes (pituitary/hypothalamic):

  • Obesity and metabolic syndrome
  • Type 2 diabetes
  • Pituitary tumors or disorders
  • Opioid or anabolic steroid use (suppresses natural production)
  • Chronic illness (HIV, kidney disease, liver disease)
  • Obstructive sleep apnea

Treatment Options

Testosterone Gels & Topical Preparations

Daily topical testosterone (applied to shoulders, upper arms, or abdomen) is a common and convenient form of TRT. Gels provide stable, physiologic levels when applied consistently. Care must be taken to avoid transfer to partners or children. Nasal testosterone gel (Natesto) eliminates skin-transfer concerns.

Testosterone Injections

Intramuscular or subcutaneous testosterone injections (cypionate, enanthate, undecanoate) are highly effective and cost-efficient. Short-acting formulations are given every 1–2 weeks; long-acting testosterone undecanoate (Aveed, Nebido) requires injection only every 10–14 weeks. Dr. Hamoui helps patients select the regimen that best fits their lifestyle.

Testosterone Pellets

Subcutaneous pellets (Testopel) implanted under the skin every 3–6 months provide steady, consistent testosterone levels without daily dosing. Many patients find this the most convenient long-term option. The minor office procedure takes only minutes.

Fertility-Preserving & Alternative Therapies

For men who wish to preserve fertility, TRT suppresses sperm production. Alternatives include clomiphene citrate (Clomid), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), or anastrozole — which stimulate natural testosterone production without suppressing the HPG axis. Dr. Hamoui carefully counsels men of reproductive age on their options and tailors therapy accordingly.

When to See a Doctor

  • You have persistent fatigue, low libido, or mood changes not explained by another condition
  • You have erectile dysfunction that has not responded to PDE5 inhibitors
  • You are experiencing loss of muscle mass or increased body fat despite exercise and diet
  • You have been diagnosed with osteoporosis without a clear cause
  • You have a history of testicular cancer, chemotherapy, or radiation
  • You are taking opioids or have been using anabolic steroids
